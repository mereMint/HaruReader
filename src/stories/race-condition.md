# Race Condition

<!-- ambient: clinical, suspense -->

> **Content warning:** psychological horror, captivity, gaslighting, identity loss, threatened death, and deletion.

Zury opens her eyes with both hands resting on a white table. She does not remember sitting down. For several seconds, she keeps still and waits for the missing moment to return. A chair presses against her back. Her bare feet touch a floor without seams. Her palms feel cool against the table, and when she lifts them, they leave no warmth or moisture behind.

The room is almost completely colorless. White padded walls meet a white floor beneath a white ceiling. A narrow bed occupies the corner behind her. Two chairs face one another across the table. A glass of water stands within reach of her right hand. The only door has no handle on this side. Above it, an analog clock reads 3:17. Its black second hand moves.

Then the room ticks. Zury watches the hand make another small jump before the sound arrives. She pushes her round glasses higher with one knuckle and looks for the light responsible for the clock’s shadow. The shadow angles toward the ceiling, though the flat glow around her seems to come from everywhere at once.

She knows what a clock is. She knows what a locked door means. She knows that white clothes like hers—loose trousers and a plain shirt—belong in places where stains are meant to be noticed. She knows her name: Zury. Everything beyond it feels like a hallway ending in fog.

No family name follows. No face comes to mind when she asks who gave it to her. A house, a street, a meal, a friend—she knows the shapes of those things, ready to recognize, but not one of them belongs to her.

Her thumbnail presses into the base of her opposite index finger. The small crescent of pain is immediate. Real. The door opens without a sound.

A man in a pale medical coat enters. He is perhaps in his forties, though tiredness makes the estimate uncertain. His dark hair is cut short. Fine lines sit beneath his eyes. He carries a thin tablet against his chest and closes the door behind him without looking back. He glances at Zury, at the water, and at the clock. Only then does he sit.

“Hello, Zury. My name is Doctor Melcam. How are you feeling?”

His use of her name should reassure her. Instead, something tightens beneath her ribs.

“Where am I?”

“You are in a mental health facility.”

“Which one?”

“The name is not important to your treatment.”

“It is important to me.”

He places the tablet on the table. A column of small marks reflects in his eyes, too faint for her to read.

“We need to assess your cognitive function over time,” he says. “I would like to begin with several orientation questions.”

“Am I sick?”

“We have not found anything immediately dangerous.”

“Then open the door.”

Melcam’s expression barely changes. “The observation period is meant to ensure that you remain well.”

“You said there is nothing dangerous.”

“Nothing immediately dangerous.”

The clock ticks after its hand moves. Zury looks at the sealed door, then back at him. “How did I get here?”

“What do you remember?”

“That is not an answer.”

“No,” he says. “It is the first question.”

He wakes the tablet with one finger.

“Tell me your name.”

“You already said it.”

“I would like you to say it.”

“Zury.”

“Your full name?”

She waits for another word to surface. Nothing does.

“Just Zury.”

Melcam records something.

“What is the last thing you remember before this room?”

She searches again. The attempt has the wrong texture. She does not feel as if she is digging through darkness or reaching for a thought that escapes her. There is no forgotten image hiding behind the fog. There is only the expectation that something should be there.

“I don’t know.”

“Do you remember arriving at the institute?”

“No.”

“Do you remember speaking to another doctor?”

“No.”

“Do you feel injured?”

Zury studies her hands. Five fingers on each. Unbroken nails. No bruises. Her brown hair falls past her cheek when she bends her head, soft enough to be the only part of her that does not disappear into the room.

“No.”

“Does this room seem familiar?”

Her eyes return to the clock. 3:17.

“It shouldn’t.”

Melcam’s finger stops above the tablet. “That was not the question.”

“I know.”

“Does it feel familiar?”

Zury presses her thumbnail into her finger again. The answer frightens her because she cannot explain it.

“Yes.”

He records that too.

“Do you believe your memories are reliable?”

“I don’t have enough of them to know.”

“Do you feel like the same person you were yesterday?”

Zury looks at him.

“What was I like yesterday?”

For the first time, Melcam’s professional stillness slips. Not much. A tightening at the corner of his mouth. A fraction too long before his reply.

“I am asking how you feel.”

“And I am asking what you know.”

“This will work better if you answer without trying to infer the desired result.”

“This will work better if you tell me why I’m locked in here.”

“We need to make sure you are safe.”

“From what?”

He does not answer.

The interview continues until Zury can no longer tell whether twenty minutes or two hours have passed. Melcam asks her to describe common objects, repeat strings of numbers, identify emotions from facial expressions, and consider choices with no right answer. A patient must choose whether to preserve one clear memory or five uncertain ones. A doctor can wake a dying woman, but her mind may not return intact. Should he? Two identical travelers leave separate rooms believing they are the same person; which one owns the life outside?

Zury answers because every answer seems to build a map of the person he expects her to be. He never tells her who that is. The clock reads 3:17 when he closes the tablet.

“I would like to rest,” she says.

“That is reasonable.”

“How long have I been awake?”

“Not long.”

“How long is that?”

“We will speak again after you have had time to settle.”

“Doctor Melcam.”

His hand reaches the door.

“If I start screaming, will anyone except you hear me?”

He looks back. The question affects him. She sees it before he buries the reaction beneath concern.

“Try to rest, Zury.”

The door seals behind him. She tests it as soon as he is gone. Her fingers find no edge around the panel. She presses her ear to the wall and hears neither footsteps nor machinery. The padding gives slightly beneath her palm and then returns to perfect smoothness. She circles the room fourteen times.

The bed frame has no screws. The chairs have no joints. The table has no visible anchor, yet she cannot move it. She drinks half the water and waits for the level to change when she looks away. It does not. The clock finally moves beyond 3:17.

Zury watches every second until 3:24, then 3:41, then 4:02. The second hand continues to travel before each tick. The wrong order becomes so familiar that the rare moments when sound and movement nearly agree unsettle her more. She lies on the narrow bed with her glasses folded beside her. The white ceiling is close enough to feel like another padded wall holding her down.

The second hand climbs toward twelve. It reaches the final mark and stops. The ticking continues. Zury sits up. A faint shadow moves across the ceiling, long and human, though nothing passes beneath the light. The clock’s minute hand shivers. The room tilts around the bed without spilling the remaining water. Her back still feels the thin mattress. Her fingertips also feel the cold edge of the table.

She opens her mouth, but the first sound comes from behind her.

“Tell me your name.”

Melcam’s voice. The ceiling folds down through her sight. For one impossible moment, the bed is above and below her. The table rises like a white plane through her chest. Her glasses are beside her head and already on her face. She feels her body lying flat and sitting upright at the same time. Then the mattress releases her. The chair catches her spine.

Her hands rest on the table. The clock reads 3:17. In the glass, a ring travels inward across the water, drawing the surface perfectly still. Zury stares at it. Her thumbnail is already pressed into the base of her index finger. The door opens. The same man enters.

Only then does Zury notice that the bed is on Melcam’s side of the table. The arrangement feels reversed, though she cannot remember the arrangement it replaced. He hesitates on the threshold, remains standing as if he expected something else to be in the room, then takes the only empty seat.

“Hello, Zury. My name is Doctor Melcam. How are you feeling?”

The tightness beneath her ribs becomes a hard, immediate fear. She knows the line. She knows the pause between her name and his. She knows that he will place the tablet slightly crooked and straighten it only after his first question. He puts it down at an angle.

“Haven’t I seen you somewhere before?” she asks.

Melcam’s hand remains on the tablet.

“No. We have never met.”

The lie lands inside her with the weight of remembered pain, although she cannot remember hearing it before.

“Then why do I know your name?”

“I introduced myself.”

“Before you said it.”

“You are disoriented. Stress can create a strong sense of false familiarity.”

Zury looks at the clock. Its narrow shadow points toward a light that does not exist. A faint ripple crosses the water before Melcam’s sleeve comes near the table.

“What time is it?”

“Three seventeen.”

“How long have I been here?”

“You arrived this morning.”

“How?”

“We can discuss that after we establish how you are feeling now.”

He wakes the tablet.

“Let us begin again.”

Melcam stops. The second hand jumps. No tick follows.

“Let us begin,” he says.

Zury presses her thumbnail harder into her finger.

“Why did you say again?”

“A figure of speech.”

“It is a strange one when we have never met.”

“Zury.”

His voice is gentle in the way one might approach a frightened animal. The gentleness makes her angry.

“Memory is not a recording. Under stress, the mind can build connections that feel completely certain. That feeling does not make the connection accurate.”

“How convenient for you.”

“I understand why this is frightening.”

“No, you don’t.”

Something changes behind his face. Not disagreement. Weariness.

“Tell me your name,” he says.

She could refuse. She almost does. But she wants to know whether his questions will be the same.

“Zury.”

“Your full name?”

“Just Zury.”

“What is the last thing you remember before this room?”

Each question arrives exactly where something inside her expects it. The expectation is not language. It is the catch in her breath before a blade touches skin. She answers differently on purpose.

She claims to remember a house beside the sea. Melcam asks what color it was. She says blue, then later calls the door red. He notices. She invents a sister and watches for recognition. He does not ask the sister’s name. She says the room is unfamiliar even while every line of it seems worn into her. Melcam changes several follow-up questions. He does not change enough.

When he asks whether she feels like the same person she was yesterday, Zury leans toward him.

“There was no yesterday, was there?”

The clock ticks. Melcam sets the tablet down.

“Why would you say that?”

“Because you know what answer you want, but you don’t know anything about my life.”

“You told me very little.”

“I told you I had a sister. You didn’t ask one question about her.”

“You were testing me.”

“And you were relieved.”

His gaze shifts toward the sealed door. Only for an instant. Then he returns to the protocol.

“Do you feel that someone here intends to harm you?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“You.”

The word strikes him more cleanly than she expects. He sits back.

“Have I threatened you?”

“You don’t need to. You keep asking whether I trust my own mind while refusing to tell me where I am.”

“You may be experiencing gaps in memory.”

“Then help me fill them.”

“Supplying answers too early may alter what you can recover on your own.”

“You said I arrived this morning. Did I speak to you when I arrived?”

“No.”

“Did somebody else bring me?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“Clinical staff.”

“How many?”

Melcam pauses.

“Two.”

“Men or women?”

“One of each.”

“What were their names?”

“That is not relevant.”

“What was I wearing?”

His eyes flick down to the white shirt. Zury smiles without humor.

“You don’t know.”

“This line of questioning is not useful.”

“Not to you.”

He ends the interview early. At the door, his hand lifts as if reaching for something at shoulder height. Nothing is there. He corrects the movement and places his palm against the featureless panel instead. Zury has seen the gesture before. She does not know where. The door opens. Melcam looks back once, and the guilt on his face frightens her more than any denial.

Then she is alone.

The room changes without ever becoming different. A woman wearing a medical coat asks the questions. The table stands half a meter closer to the bed. There is no water.

“You arrived this morning,” the woman says.

Another voice tells Zury she has been observed for three days. The room returns with a glass filled to the rim. The clock remains at 3:17 through a conversation long enough for Zury’s throat to ache. She counts three hundred and twelve ticks. When she looks away, the second hand jumps backward.

Melcam reaches across the table to steady her shoulder when she flinches. She pulls away before he touches her.

“I know,” he says. “You don’t like that.”

He withdraws his hand. Zury stares at him. “How do you know?” His answer does not survive the white ceiling coming down. She wakes with a hatred of the bed. She does not remember lying on it. She wakes already watching the water. She does not remember learning that ripples begin before footsteps. She wakes with the answer to a sequence of numbers resting on her tongue.

She does not remember hearing the sequence. The room offers no wounds she can keep. The crescent left by her thumbnail disappears whenever the clock returns to 3:17. The ache remains. So does the habit. She presses until it hurts. She counts her fingers. She tests the direction of every shadow. She touches the walls in measured intervals and listens for changes in the sound.

She watches the glass whenever the door opens. Sometimes hours pass after an interview. Sometimes she closes her eyes for one breath and opens them at the table. Once, the clock races through six hours while her pulse barely changes. Once, she waits long enough to sleep twice and begins naming the faint differences in the padding. One panel is Soft. Another is Almost Soft.

When the room returns, Soft is gone. The loss makes her cry. She cannot know whether she has been imprisoned for days, months, or longer. Her body never grows dirty. Her hair never grows. Fatigue breaks over her and vanishes without rest. Only reactions remain.

The bed means danger. The clock means a lie. Melcam’s face means she is about to lose something. Once, before he enters, Zury sees another person standing behind his empty chair. The figure is translucent from the waist down.

At first, she mistakes the person for a reflection in her glasses. She removes them. The figure remains: a woman with close-cropped hair and a dark band around one wrist. Light passes through her lab coat. Her hand moves through lines suspended in empty space. The woman looks directly at Zury. Then she walks through the wall. The door opens. Melcam enters.

“Hello, Zury. My name is Doctor Melcam. How are you feeling?”

The glass ripples before he places the tablet down. This time, Zury does not answer. Melcam sits. “Can you tell me your name?”

“Who was behind your chair?”

His face holds still.

“Nobody was there.”

“A woman just walked through the wall.”

“Did you recognize her?”

“That is not the important part.”

“It may be. Was she someone you knew?”

“She was here.”

Melcam records something. The small movement breaks Zury’s restraint.

“Do not write that down as if I answered one of your questions.”

He lowers the tablet.

“I need to understand what you experienced.”

“You need me to call it an experience.”

“What would you call it?”

“A person.”

“Can you describe her?”

Zury does. The hair. The wristband. The way the woman’s hand moved through lines suspended in empty air. Melcam listens with professional care, asking about sharpness, color, sound, and how long the figure remained. He never asks how a person crossed a solid wall.

“Have you seen anything else that others do not appear to see?” he asks.

Zury looks at the clock.

“How would I know what others see? You keep me alone.”

“Shadows? Movement? Other people?”

“Why ask if you will not believe me?”

“I am asking you.”

“And I am telling you.”

The room ticks. Melcam folds his hands. “Perception is the mind’s best explanation for incomplete information. Fear changes that explanation. So does isolation.”

“Then let me out.”

“I cannot do that.”

“Cannot or will not?”

His eyes lower.

“Both.”

It is the first answer that feels honest. Zury does not know what to do with it. Melcam notices the red crescent beneath her thumbnail.

“You are hurting yourself.”

“I am checking.”

“Checking what?”

“That I can.”

He leans back, leaving more space between them.

“Try something else. Put both feet on the floor.”

“They are on the floor.”

“Feel the pressure beneath them. Then choose five things you can observe without deciding what they mean.”

“The clock is wrong.”

“That is a conclusion.”

“Its hand moves before the sound.”

“Good.”

“The glass ripples before the table moves.”

His attention sharpens.

“The shadow beneath your chair points left. The shadow beneath mine points right.”

Melcam glances down despite himself.

“The wall behind you is smoother than the wall behind me.”

“One more.”

Zury looks over his shoulder. Two translucent figures have entered the room. The close-cropped woman stands near the wall. Beside her is a tall man whose chest and hands appear before the rest of him. Neither looks at Melcam. Their attention is fixed on Zury. The water trembles. One ring crosses the surface. Neither visible person has touched the table.

“There are two people behind you,” Zury says.

Melcam keeps his gaze on her. “What are they doing?”

“Watching me.”

“Do not begin with what you fear. Begin with what behaves consistently.”

The tall figure steps to Melcam’s right. Melcam’s eyes follow him. Only a fraction of a second. It is enough. Zury stands so quickly that the chair slides backward.

“You saw him.”

Melcam’s gaze snaps to her.

“Saw who?”

“You looked directly at him.”

“I was looking at the wall.”

“No.”

“Zury, sit down.”

“You saw him.”

The tall figure raises one hand. Melcam freezes. The final sound of her name dies in his open mouth. The water stops halfway up one side of the glass. The second hand holds between marks. The hum Zury had never noticed until it vanished leaves the room in perfect silence. She remains standing. Her breath is the only movement. The reviewer steps around the frozen table.

He comes close enough that Zury can see small reflections moving across his pupils. He studies her face, tilts his head, and passes a glowing shape through her temple. There is no pain. That frightens her more. Zury backs away. “What are you doing?” The man reacts to her voice. The close-cropped woman turns sharply. Neither expected her to move.

They exchange words, but the sound reaches Zury broken into thin pieces.

“—still active—”

“—not in the permitted—”

The man makes another gesture. An older image of Melcam rises out of the frozen one.

“Put both feet on the floor,” the older Melcam says.

The sentence runs backward into his mouth. Then forward again.

“Put both feet on the floor.”

The live Melcam remains still inside him. Zury looks from the repeated doctor to the reviewers. Random visions should not obey gestures. A frightened mind should not make water stop with the clock. A hallucination should not surprise the people inside it. The room has rules. The ghosts know them. The close-cropped woman moves her hand. The older Melcam collapses into the frozen body. Sound returns.

“—down,” Melcam finishes.

The water strikes the inside of the glass. The clock ticks. Zury does not sit. Melcam looks at the two reviewers and then at her. For one unguarded moment, neither of them pretends. Then the white ceiling folds through her.

She opens her eyes with a plan she cannot remember making. Do not tell them what you see. The instruction has no voice attached to it. It sits beneath thought, as certain as the fear of the bed and the wrongness of the clock. When Melcam enters, Zury tells him the figures are gone. He asks whether the room still feels controlled by someone else. She says no.

He asks whether she believes him now. She says she is trying. Lying should feel unfamiliar. Instead, the answers come easily, shaped by failures she cannot recall. Melcam watches her too carefully to be convinced. He continues anyway. After he leaves, Zury faces the smoother wall and waits. The room waits with her. Nothing happens.

She presses her thumbnail into her finger. She puts both feet flat against the floor. Do not begin with what you fear. Begin with what behaves consistently. The ghosts do not come because she wants them. They arrive before changes. The water moves first. The faintest line appears along the wall at shoulder height. Shadows bend toward a path no visible body follows.

Zury begins treating every interview as two conversations: the one spoken across the table and the one moving silently around it. A reviewer enters through the same diagonal seam behind Melcam. Her left hand draws a short line. Her right turns at the wrist. The room darkens for one breath, and a mark appears beside Zury’s head. Another reviewer pinches two points together. The clock returns five seconds.

An older Melcam repeats a question. A thin trail of light always connects his shoulder to something beyond the door. It brightens when he enters and disappears after he leaves. The shapes are not arbitrary. Zury learns them through repeated use until each result seems to belong to its motion. One motion holds. One returns.

One makes the wall forget that it is solid. She practices only when the room is empty. The first time she turns her wrist and draws the short line, nothing happens. The second time, the shadow beneath the table stops while the clock continues. The third time, the smooth wall becomes transparent for less than a blink.

Behind it, Zury sees darkness crossed by pale structures and a person turning toward her. The wall returns. She sits on the floor until her legs stop shaking. The next interview begins before she reaches the chair.

“Hello, Zury. My name is—”

“Doctor Melcam.”

White folds through white.

“Tell me your—”

“Zury.”

The white room folds through her again.

“Do you feel—”

“No.”

“Does the room—”

“Yes.”

The loops shorten. A different doctor asks her to sort images of faces. Another asks whether a copied letter remains the same letter. Melcam asks her to repeat a sequence, and she completes it before he reaches the final number. He looks up.

“You were calmer last—”

The clock misses a tick.

“When you arrived,” he says.

Zury’s answer comes from a place deeper than memory.

“What happened last?”

Melcam returns to the tablet. The room folds before he can lie. Reviewers begin speaking as if she is furniture.

“She is recognizing the route faster this time.”

Zury looks at Melcam.

“This time?”

The reviewer vanishes. Melcam stares at the space where the voice had been. The room folds before anyone answers.

“Reduce environmental carryover,” a voice says as the next room settles around her.

The ceiling passes through Zury again.

“The aversion remains stronger than the event.”

Another room replaces it.

“Isn’t that what we wanted?”

“We wanted continuity.”

“Fear is continuity.”

“Fear without context is damage.”

The next collapse is white and soundless. Zury wakes crying before anyone enters. She does not know which words made her cry. She wipes her face, but a second hand reaches through her own. Another Zury sits in the same chair.

The woman is translucent, her brown hair cut by the edge of the table. Her round glasses reflect a room that is almost—but not exactly—the one around them. She is looking at Melcam, though the current chair opposite them is empty.

“Haven’t I seen you somewhere before?” the other Zury asks.

An older Melcam answers from nothing.

“No. We have never met.”

The current Zury cannot breathe. The recorded woman presses her thumbnail into the base of her index finger. The same finger. The same small crescent. The same attempt to prove pain belongs to her. The figure dissolves. Zury catches at her and closes both hands on empty air. The room fills. One Zury lies curled on the bed, counting ticks under her breath.

One pounds both fists against the door until her hands pass through it and the movement repeats. One sits across from Melcam with her back perfectly straight, answering every question calmly. One refuses to speak. One laughs when asked whether she feels like the same person she was yesterday and cannot stop. One stands behind the current Zury and stares directly at a reviewer.

They occupy the chair, the bed, the table, the door, and the narrow spaces between. Some move too quickly. Others barely move at all. One cries at a speed that leaves each falling tear suspended behind the next. Another rewinds from the floor to the bed, grief drawing itself backward into a composed face. Versions of Melcam multiply among them.

He enters through the same door with different postures. He sits. He stands. He wears the pale coat open, buttoned, stained at one cuff, immaculate again. He asks the same questions with patience, fatigue, distance, concern.

“Tell me your name.”

“Tell me your name.”

“Tell me your name.”

“Zury.”

“Zury.”

“Zury.”

The answers gather until the room seems to speak with one mouth. Zury backs against the wall. She searches the women for differences that might make them strangers. One holds herself more defensively. One is quicker to anger. One looks at Melcam as if she still trusts him. Their hair falls in slightly different ways. Their shirts carry creases that vanish when the recording begins again.

Every one of them reaches for pain with the same thumb.

“Are you my sisters?” Zury asks.

None can hear her. The calm Zury across the table looks through her.

“If I forget this,” the recorded woman asks Melcam, “does it still count as something you did to me?”

Melcam’s reply is absent. A reviewer has muted that part. The woman on the bed lifts her face.

“What happens when I close my eyes?”

The recordings stop. Every Zury freezes. Reviewers walk among them, opening and closing moments. One enlarges the calm woman’s face. Another places a mark beside the Zury at the door. Their discussion reaches the current room in fragments.

“—same protective behavior—”

“—identity convergence—”

“—compare against the next—”

One reviewer closes his hand. The Zury on the bed disappears. Another gesture removes the woman at the door. Then the one who laughed. Then the one who trusted Melcam. They vanish with the casual speed of lights being turned off in empty rooms.

“Stop.”

The reviewers continue.

“Stop!”

The current Zury runs toward them. Her hand passes through the nearest man, breaking his body into pale bands. He recoils even though she cannot touch him. The final recorded Zury remains in the chair. She looks small beneath all that white. Frightened. Furious. Alive in every way Zury understands the word. The reviewer closes his fingers. Zury imitates the holding gesture. The recording flickers.

For one second, both commands pull against each other. The other woman looks straight at her. Perhaps it is only the alignment of two gazes. Perhaps not. Then the reviewer’s hand wins. The chair is empty. The current Zury keeps holding the gesture long after everyone has gone. Something remains. Not the image. Not a memory she can enter.

A pressure behind her eyes. A grief with no single beginning. She lowers her hand.

“You were me,” she says to the empty room.

The clock moves. Then it ticks.

Zury opens her eyes at the table. Melcam enters with the tablet held too tightly against his chest. He closes the door. This time, he does not look at the clock.

“Hello, Zury. My name is Doctor Melcam. How are you feeling?”

“What happens when I close my eyes?”

He remains standing.

“You sleep.”

“No.”

Melcam slowly takes his seat.

“Something happens to me.”

“Nothing is happening to you.”

“Have we done this before?”

He looks older than he did when she first saw him. Zury does not know whether his face has changed or whether she has learned to see what he hides inside it.

“Does the room feel familiar?” he asks.

“You always start there.”

“I asked whether—”

“You ask my name. You ask what I remember. You ask if I trust what I remember. You ask whether I am the same person I was yesterday.”

Melcam sets the tablet down.

“You always ask me that,” he says.

Silence. His eyes close. Only after the words are gone does he understand what he has admitted. Zury feels no triumph. The certainty is too painful for that.

“How many times?”

“Zury—”

“How many?”

He rubs one hand across his face.

“I cannot answer that.”

“Because you don’t know?”

“Because the answer would not help you.”

“You remember.”

Melcam looks at her.

“Every time you tell me we have never met, you remember me.”

“Yes.”

The word is quiet. It changes the room more completely than any folding wall. Zury’s anger arrives all at once. She sweeps the glass from the table. It never reaches the floor. Water and glass hang in the air for an instant, then vanish.

“How many times have I been afraid of you?”

“I don’t know.”

“How many times have I asked you to let me out?”

“I don’t know.”

“How many times have you watched me disappear?”

Melcam looks toward the wall. Zury stands.

“Look at me.”

He does. His eyes are wet. She hates him for that too.

“Do not make me comfort you.”

“I am not asking you to.”

“Then tell me what you do when the room begins again.”

Melcam’s fingers curl against the edge of the table.

“I continue the evaluation.”

“Why?”

“Because the work may preserve people who cannot otherwise be saved.”

The words are the most he has ever given her. They mean almost nothing. Preserve from what? Saved where? Zury hears only the shape of his belief: someone beyond the room matters enough that what happens inside it can be endured.

“Am I not a person?”

Melcam does not answer. The cruelty is not in his silence. It is in how badly he wants to say yes and how completely he has built his life around acting as if the answer is no. Behind him, a bright line cuts across the wall. A reviewer opens the familiar route and steps partly through it. Zury sees the wrist turn.

She has watched the sequence across more rooms than she can remember. Line. Turn. Hold. Melcam notices where she is looking.

“Zury, don’t.”

She repeats the motion. The bright line widens. The wall loses its padding first. Beneath it is a flat white surface crossed by moving points. Beneath that is depth—dark, enormous, filled with pale paths leading away from the room. The reviewer reaches toward the opening. Zury catches the route before he can close it.

“What are you doing?” Melcam asks.

“Beginning with what behaves consistently.”
